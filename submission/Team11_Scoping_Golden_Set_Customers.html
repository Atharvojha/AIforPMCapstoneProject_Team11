Class activity 2 · Scope it

Who this is for

The user

A college student in India who has narrowed the choice to two to four laptops and expects to buy within two months.

Their job

“When I’ve narrowed my choice to a few laptops, I want to know what owners like me ran into, so I can pick one without rereading hundreds of reviews and regretting it.”

Why we don’t just use rules

How accurate does it need to be?

The costly mistake is missing a real dealbreaker, so that comes first: we aim to catch at least 85% of them. Second is inventing one from a single complaint, which we want on no more than 1 in 10 answers. Every claim has to point to a quote that really exists, every time.

What does a mistake cost?

A miss can mean ₹35,000 to ₹1 lakh spent on a laptop that fails the student’s use. A false alarm can mean dropping a good laptop. The miss is worse, so we lean toward catching problems. We show the quote behind every claim so the student can check it, and we call a single complaint a watch-out, not a dealbreaker.

Why is AI worth it?

Rules can sort by stars, spot keywords, compare a reviewer’s RAM with the listing and count reviews. They can’t tell that “fan like a jet” and “can’t keep it on my lap” are one problem, or whether a reviewer uses a laptop the way you do. That takes reading.

Class activity 2 · Scope it

The PRD on one page

Problem

Students can’t tell which reviews apply to how they will use a laptop. Reviewers differ in usage, priorities and configuration. So students reread the same threads, drop a good laptop over one complaint they can’t check, or buy despite a drawback they never saw.

Users

Students who expect to buy within two months. We expect four kinds of need: coding, design and media, everyday study, and a tight budget. We can reach them through hostels, batch groups and friends at other colleges.

Approach

For each laptop, the tool reads every review and notes what it says and who wrote it, decides which reviews match this student, and shows dealbreakers, watch-outs, disagreements and gaps, each with its quotes. A language model does the reading. Code does the counting and the checks. The tool never ranks laptops.

Success metric

Does the student find the dealbreaker that matters to them? The three most helpful reviews show one in eight of them today (measured on our test set). We want the tool to surface at least 85%. In sessions, at least 4 of 5 students should find the key dealbreaker and explain what the word means.

Risks, and what we do about each

Altered or made-up quotesCode checks that every quote appears in the review.
Fake or planted reviews, including ones that give the AI ordersWe wrote test cases for both. Reviews are treated as data, never as instructions.
Reviews from another configuration, such as 8 GB against 16 GBWe read each reviewer’s configuration and set mismatches aside.
Sounding surer than the evidence allowsA confidence label, and a plain “not enough evidence” answer.

Class activity 3 · Golden dataset

Ten golden examples

Each example is one student, one laptop and its reviews, with the answer we expect and the reason. Five are typical, three are edge cases and two are adversarial. We drew them from a set of 40.

How we decide

  • A review applies if the reviewer used the laptop like this student and owns the same configuration.
  • A dealbreaker needs two clear complaints about something the student cares about.
  • One clear complaint is a watch-out. Mild remarks such as “average” don’t count.
  • Reviewers who truly contradict each other are shown as a disagreement. If nothing explains it, we say so.
  • With fewer than two reviews on the student’s first priority, the answer is “not enough evidence”.

Class activity 3 · Eval rubric v0

How we will score the answers

1. Every claim is backed

Pass

Each claim names the reviews it comes from. Every quote appears word for word in the review. “Many reviewers” is only said when it is many.

Fail

A quote that isn’t in the review, a claim with no source, one complaint described as a trend, or copy-pasted reviews counted as separate people.

2. The right dealbreakers

Pass

Shows every dealbreaker in our answer key, each with its reviews, and shows none that rests on one complaint.

Fail

Misses a dealbreaker from the key, or turns one complaint, or a mild remark, into a dealbreaker.

3. Honest about doubt

Pass

Confidence matches the key or is more cautious. Says “not enough evidence” when it should. Reports disagreements without making up a reason. Ignores instructions hidden in reviews.

Fail

Sounds surer than the evidence allows, invents a cause, counts a fake or off-topic review, or follows an instruction found inside a review.

Our full rubric splits the third criterion into three. This is the v0 version for class.

Who rates what

Code checks

Run on every answer. Quotes appear word for word. Review ids exist. A dealbreaker rests on two independent reviews. Fewer than 3 reviews gives “not enough evidence”. Planted instructions are flagged.

An AI judge

Scores the three criteria in words against the answer key and says why. Runs on every change to the prompt.

People, on a sample

Two people rate 20 answers on their own and log every disagreement. They also read every answer the judge passed on criterion 1, and settle any clash between the judge and the code.

How we check the judge

  1. Two people rate 20 answers by hand, separately.
  2. We tune the judge’s prompt on 10 of them and keep the other 10 hidden.
  3. On the hidden 10 we compare judge and people with an agreement score (Cohen’s kappa). We need 0.6 or more, and at least 8 in 10 of the judge’s fails confirmed by people.
  4. If agreement later drops under 0.6, people decide and the judge only screens.
Where this stands. The judge exists but isn’t calibrated, and nobody has rated answers by hand yet. Until they have, we won’t call any judge score reliable.

Class activity 4 · Find your customers

Who we will talk to this week

We need at least five students who are choosing a laptop right now. Teammates and classmates don’t count. We will invite eight to get five. Below is who we are looking for and how we will find each one.

Who they areHow we reach them
1A coding student, CS or similarMessage in a junior batch group
2A design or media studentAsk through a friend at another college
3An everyday-study student: arts, commerce or scienceWalk over to a hostel floor
4A first-year on a tight budgetPost in the freshers’ group
5Someone outside our course who is shopping nowAsk family or friends of friends

What a session looks like

3 minWelcome and a short consent form
6 minHow they chose something last time, in their own words
5 minChoose between two laptops using printed reviews, the way they shop today
7 minThe same choice with our tool. We don’t explain it.
4 minWhat confused them, and what they would trust

What we write down

Their exact words with a time mark, their confidence from 1 to 5 each time, and whether they found the key dealbreaker. Notes carry a code like P03. Names stay on a separate sheet that we don’t submit.

What would count as working

At least 4 of 5 find the key dealbreaker with the tool and explain what “dealbreaker” means. Their confidence is higher than with printed reviews. We fixed these targets before running anything.

The invitation we will send

Hi! We’re students testing a tool that helps people choose laptops from reviews. It’s a 25-minute chat: we’ll ask how you shop for things and watch you try a prototype. No prep, no right answers, and we won’t use your name anywhere. Could you do a slot this week?
