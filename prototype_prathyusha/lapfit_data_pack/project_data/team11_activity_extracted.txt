A weekend we keep hearing about (illustrative; our
sessions will test it). A second-year CS student in
Hyderabad has ₹60,000 from home and four laptops on the
list. Fourteen browser tabs in, one owner says the battery
lasts “5-6 hours” and another says about an hour and a
half. Half the reviewers are gamers, some own a different
RAM version, and a parent wants an answer by Sunday.

The problem
P R O B L E M S TAT E M E N T

Students buying a laptop for study cannot tell which of the many reviews apply to how they will use it, so
they miss a dealbreaker or reject a good option over one unverified complaint. Why now: language models
can now read and cite across a whole review set. (Assumption: our customer sessions will confirm that review
overload is a real, current pain.)

How good it has to be
How accurate does it
need to be?

The costly mistake is missing a real dealbreaker, so that comes first: we aim to catch at least
85% of them, which is 14 of the 16 in our test key. Second is inventing one from a single
complaint, which we want on no more than 1 in 10 answers, that is 4 of 40. Every claim has
to point to a quote that really exists, every time. So far that holds: all 40 test answers kept
every quote word for word.

What does a mistake
cost?

A miss can mean ₹35,000 to ₹1 lakh spent on a laptop that fails the student’s use (an
assumption we haven’t checked). Reviewers in our own data paid ₹28,000 for an ASUS
VivoBook 15 (FK-01382) and ₹58,000 for an HP Pavilion Gaming (FK-02936), so the range
is plausible. A false alarm can mean dropping a good laptop. The miss is worse, so we lean
toward catching problems. We show the quote behind every claim so the student can check
it, and we call a single complaint a watch-out, not a dealbreaker.

Why is AI worth it?

Rules can sort by stars, count reviews and check quotes, but they can’t read. Reading is the
only thing we hand to the AI, and code checks its work. The task-by-task split is on the next
page.

LIMIT WE FOUND WHEN WE CRITIQUED OUR OWN
PRD

W H AT W E A R E D O I N G A B O U T I T

1

Our data is not India long-form. Flipkart reviews are short
(median about 9 words) and about 65% are 5-star, with no
dates or variant field. The Amazon set is US data: median
72 words, 6% from refurbished listings.

We report every metric by source, state the limit in the PRD, and
collect the review pages our five students actually read.

2

We write our own labels, so they can carry our bias, and the
40-case key was drafted with AI help.

Two of us rate each example independently and log every
disagreement. A person checks ten of the AI-drafted labels. We
never edit the set after it is frozen to raise a score.

3

“Applies to me” needs a clear use. A vague input such as
“for college” gives a vague answer.

The student picks one of 12 preset uses before any result (FR1).
Free-text needs are future scope.

4

We do not detect fake or paid reviews.

It is a non-goal and a risk for v0. We show the source and review
count behind each claim, and copied reviews count once.

5

We have not shown we beat what retailers already offer,
such as Amazon’s “Customers say” summary. We checked
only a thin slice of the literature.

In iteration 1 we run both on the same listings and compare the
answers, and ask students in the sessions.

G U A R D R A I L S B U I LT I N T O T H E P R O D U C T

Zero fabricated quotes. A quote checker runs on every
output, with an alert on any miss.

Reviews are data, never instructions. A review that gives
orders is flagged and left out (example 10).

One complaint is never a dealbreaker. Two clear, applicable
complaints are needed; one is a watch-out.

No ranking, no score, no verdict. The student decides.
If analysis fails, show the raw reviews. Never a half answer.

“Not enough evidence” is a real answer. Silence is never
read as “looks fine”.

PROBLEM AND CONTEXT

Background. Students skim star averages and top reviews. Pages sort
by votes, not relevance (A: not yet observed).
The problem. They can’t tell which reviews are about them, or a

Why now. Models can read and cite across a whole review set,
including messy, code-mixed reviews. Our prototype kept every quote
word for word on all 40 test cases.
Business impact. A wrong buy costs ₹35,000 to ₹1 lakh (A; our
reviewers paid ₹28,000 to ₹58,000). Review pages lose trust.

HOW WE DECIDE

A review applies if the reviewer used the laptop like this student and owns the same configuration.
A dealbreaker needs two clear complaints about something the student cares about.
One clear complaint is a watch-out. Mild remarks such as “average” don’t count.
Reviewers who truly contradict each other are shown as a disagreement. If nothing explains it, we say so.
With fewer than two reviews on the student’s first priority, the answer is “not enough evidence”.
